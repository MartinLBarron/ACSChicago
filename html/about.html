About the project

Understanding rent in Chicago.

Rent shapes who can afford to live in Chicago and how residents experience the city. Public data can help us understand that landscape, but it often arrives in large tables, unfamiliar geographies and technical documentation. Chicago Rent brings those sources together in a form that residents can explore and question.

The dashboard lets readers examine differences across neighborhoods, compare Chicago with other cities and follow changes across three five-year periods. The goal is to make the evidence accessible while keeping its uncertainty visible.

The research question

The Model page explores the question: is violent crime in an area associated with variations in rent prices in those areas? The analysis combines American Community Survey estimates with Chicago Police Department records. A sequence of regression models starts with crime alone, then adds groups of controls. Comparing those models helps distinguish the broad geographic association from the association that remains after accounting for measured differences between areas.

In brief, the crime association becomes much smaller as those controls enter the models. Its size and statistical uncertainty depend on the specification and method used. The Model page presents the estimates, diagnostics and spatial robustness checks.

A meta question: conducting research with AI

This project is also an experiment in working with AI agents on an extended analytical task. Agents helped write and revise R code, assemble data pipelines, build the website, fit models and draft explanations. The question is not simply whether an agent can produce an analysis, but what makes that analysis understandable, reproducible and trustworthy.

There are clear opportunities. An agent can help connect documentation, code, statistical output and a public-facing interface. It can make it easier to explore alternative specifications, automate repeated work and turn technical results into material that more people can read.

There are also weaknesses. An agent can choose an inappropriate variable, misread a table definition, introduce a coding error or describe a result more confidently than the evidence warrants. Plausible prose and polished charts can hide those problems.

For that reason, human judgment remains central: defining the question, choosing the comparisons, assessing assumptions and deciding what conclusions are justified. Reproducible code, preserved source data, explicit definitions and checks against documentation and model output make the work easier to audit. They support that judgment; they do not replace it.

A project that can be questioned and revised

Chicago Rent is developed by Martin Barron as a public research and portfolio project. It brings together data engineering, statistical analysis and visual communication, while examining how AI assistance changes each part of that process.

The work is iterative. Decisions about geography, missing estimates, variable selection and uncertainty affect the story the data can tell. As those decisions are reviewed, the analysis and its presentation may change. Making those choices inspectable is part of the project’s purpose.
