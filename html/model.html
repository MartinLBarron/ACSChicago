01 / Summary

Rent and violent crime in context.

Does violent crime help explain differences in Chicago rents? We use regression analysis to compare rents across block groups while accounting for housing characteristics, socioeconomic conditions, and community location. Higher crime counts are associated with lower rents when modeled alone, but the association become much smaller after other factors are considered. The fully adjusted models suggest a negligible impact of crime on rent.

Research question

What is the association between median contract rent and violent crime across Chicago block groups, and how does that association change after accounting for housing, socioeconomic conditions, and community-area location?

Models built

Five nested linear models use the natural log of median monthly contract rent, with equal weight per block group. M0 is the intercept-only baseline; M1 adds violent crime; M2 adds housing and renter profile; M3 adds socioeconomic context; M4 adds indicators for the 77 community areas. The models omit the college-educated share and the 20+-unit-building share.

Data used

ACS estimates describe housing composition, renter profiles, income, poverty, education and racial/ethnic composition. CPD records supply the five-year count of violent crimes located within each block group. More data details can be found under About the data.

02 / Model specifications and fit

Model specifications and fit.

We build five models in sequence to examine how the association between violent crime and rent changes as additional characteristics are considered. Every model uses the same block groups, the natural log of median monthly contract rent as its outcome, and equal weight per block group. Each stage retains all variables from the preceding stage.

04 / Diagnostics and sensitivity

Diagnostics and sensitivity checks.

M4 leaves meaningful variation unexplained. The residuals show unequal variance, heavy tails and remaining nonlinearity. These checks explain why the current model is a starting point.

Observed versus fitted rent

A perfect fit would lie on the diagonal. Fitted dollars are exponentiated log predictions, not unbiased predictions of arithmetic rent. Each point is a block group.

Residuals versus fitted rent

Zero means observed and fitted log rent agree. The red line connects mean residuals in 18 groups of similar size ordered by fitted rent. Changing spread and curvature flag unequal variance and an imperfect functional form.

Do the residuals have heavy tails?

This normal Q–Q plot compares standardized residuals with normal quantiles. Departures at the ends reveal unusually large positive and negative residuals. Saturated singleton observations are omitted.

How reliable are the rent estimates?

The published 90% margin of error divided by rent varies substantially. Above the dashed line, the MOE exceeds half the estimate. Open-ended medians without MOEs are omitted.

Spatial pattern left in the residuals

Moran’s I measures whether nearby values are similar. Community indicators reduce residual clustering substantially; some remains under adjacent-polygon weights.

05 / Methods and limitations

Methods and limitations.

The data and sample

Crime is the raw count of CPD Index I violent-crime rows dated 2020–2024 within each whole block group: homicide, criminal sexual assault, robbery and aggravated assault/battery. Records without usable coordinates are excluded from spatial counts. Counts are not population-adjusted; they also reflect area size, activity and reporting. Approximate police locations and uneven coordinate coverage can affect comparisons.

We retain whole block groups with at least half their total polygon area inside Chicago and assign each to its largest-overlap community area. Estimates are not clipped to city boundaries. Missing inputs are excluded, not imputed. The complete-case sample is held fixed after removing the two predictors, so model comparisons use the same areas.

What “neighborhood” means here

The 77 community areas have separate intercepts, with common covariate slopes. Their added fit describes location association after the measured controls. It can reflect amenities, housing quality, subsidy status, residential sorting and other omitted factors.

Income and demographics describe all relevant households or residents, not just renters. Racial composition describes social context, not individual or biological effects.

Comparisons and uncertainty

Housing shares use all renter-occupied units. Recent-mover share (moved in 2020 or later) and renter-householder shares under 35 and age 65+ enter every model from M2 onward. The omitted renter-age group is 35–64; recent moves are a stock measure, not an annual turnover rate. Two-bedroom rentals and buildings constructed in 1940–1999 are omitted housing categories. Education is represented by the no diploma/GED share among adults 25+. College-educated share is omitted. The building-size reference group includes one-unit and 20+-unit buildings, plus mobile/other structures.

Coefficient intervals are 95%; ACS MOEs are 90%. HC3 handles unequal variance but does not fix nonlinearity, spatial dependence or ACS measurement error. Community-clustered intervals are a sensitivity and can still miss dependence across adjacent communities.

Limits on interpretation

The held-out assessment keeps tracts together and evaluates communities represented in training. Recent moves and renter age were selected after exploratory use of these same folds, so the updated scores are comparative diagnostics rather than independent validation. It is not a test of prediction in new communities or future years. All results concern area medians, so they cannot establish what raises an individual tenant’s rent.
